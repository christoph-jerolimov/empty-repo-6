## Hello, World!

Classic line-numbered BASIC, as found on most 1970s and 1980s machines:

```vb
10 PRINT "Hello, World!"
20 END
```

In a modern structured dialect such as FreeBASIC or QB64 line numbers are optional:

```vb
PRINT "Hello, World!"
```

## About

BASIC ("Beginner's All-purpose Symbolic Instruction Code") was designed to be learned by non-science students in a single session. Its English keywords, immediate feedback and forgiving nature made it the language in which an entire generation first encountered programming. The early dialects relied on `GOTO` and line numbers, but later versions added structured control flow, procedures and eventually objects, leading to commercially important descendants such as Visual Basic and VBA.

## History

John Kemeny and Thomas Kurtz ran the first BASIC program at Dartmouth College on 1 May 1964 on the Dartmouth Time-Sharing System. Microsoft's first product, Altair BASIC (1975), and the ROM BASICs of the Apple II, Commodore 64, ZX Spectrum and BBC Micro made it ubiquitous in the home-computer era. Structured dialects such as QuickBASIC and the 1991 ISO Full BASIC standard modernised it, and Visual Basic (1991) carried the name into Windows GUI development. Kemeny and Kurtz themselves launched True BASIC in 1985 to preserve their original vision of a simple, portable language.
